> **Important:** This page is for general information only. Your radiation oncology team will design a treatment plan that is specific to your cancer type, stage, and anatomy. Always follow your doctor's guidance, and never adjust or stop treatment on your own.

## What is radiation therapy?

Radiation therapy — sometimes called radiotherapy or RT — is a cancer treatment that uses high-energy beams to damage cancer cells so they stop growing and dividing. Over time, the damaged cells break down and the body removes them.

It is one of the most common cancer treatments, used in about half of all cancer cases worldwide. Radiation therapy can be the main treatment, or it can be used before surgery (to shrink a tumour), after surgery (to destroy any remaining cancer cells), or alongside chemotherapy to make both treatments more effective.

## Is radiation therapy safe? Addressing common concerns

Many patients in India — and their families — have heard that radiation is dangerous or harmful to those nearby. It is worth addressing these concerns directly.

**Modern radiation therapy is carefully planned.** A radiation oncology team uses detailed imaging — usually a CT scan — to map the exact shape and position of the cancer. The treatment is designed to focus beams on the cancer area while limiting exposure to surrounding healthy tissue. The planning process typically takes several days and involves a physicist, a dosimetrist, and a radiation oncologist working together.

**External beam radiation does not make you radioactive.** The most common type of radiation therapy — where a machine directs beams at your body from outside — does not leave radiation inside you. After each session, it is completely safe to be around family members, including children. You do not need to isolate yourself, avoid physical contact, or stay away from others after treatment.

Internal radiation (brachytherapy), where a radioactive source is placed inside or near the tumour, is different — but your team will give you specific guidance if this applies to your treatment.

## Why radiation therapy is used

Radiation therapy is used for different purposes depending on the cancer and its stage:

- **To cure cancer** — in some cancers (such as early-stage cervical cancer, head and neck cancers, and certain lymphomas), radiation therapy alone can eliminate the disease.
- **To shrink a tumour before surgery** — called neoadjuvant radiation, this can make surgery less extensive and improve outcomes.
- **To destroy remaining cancer cells after surgery** — called adjuvant radiation, this lowers the chance of the cancer coming back.
- **To control cancer that cannot be removed** — radiation can slow growth, reduce tumour size, and relieve symptoms.
- **To ease symptoms** — radiation is often highly effective at relieving bone pain, reducing pressure from a tumour, or controlling bleeding caused by cancer.

Your oncologist will explain which of these goals applies to you.

## The treatment process: what happens step by step

Understanding the process ahead of time reduces anxiety significantly. Here is what most patients can expect.

### 1. Consultation

Your oncologist will review your diagnosis, scan results, and medical history. They will explain whether radiation is recommended, what the goals of treatment are, and what side effects to expect based on the area being treated. This is the time to ask questions — bring a family member or trusted person to help you remember what was said.

### 2. Simulation (planning CT scan)

Before treatment starts, you will have a planning session called a simulation. You will lie in the same position you will use during treatment while a CT scan maps your anatomy precisely. This is not a treatment session — no radiation is given. It may take 30 to 60 minutes. The team may use cushions, moulds, or a mesh mask (for head and neck treatments) to ensure you lie in exactly the same position each day.

### 3. Treatment planning

After simulation, your care team spends several days — sometimes up to two weeks — designing your treatment plan. This involves calculating the exact angles, doses, and beam shapes needed to target the cancer while protecting nearby organs as much as possible. You will not need to come in during this period.

### 4. Skin marking and verification

Before your first treatment, small ink marks or, in some centres, tiny permanent tattoo dots are placed on your skin. These ensure the beams are aimed at exactly the right spot every day. Do not wash these marks off — tell your care team if they fade and need to be refreshed.

### 5. Daily treatment sessions

Each treatment session (fraction) is short — usually 10 to 30 minutes total. Most of that time is spent positioning you correctly; the actual delivery of radiation typically takes only 1 to 5 minutes. The machine may rotate around you and may make sounds (clicking, humming, or whirring) — this is normal. The treatment itself is usually painless. You will not feel the radiation beams.

The radiation team watches you on a camera from a control room just outside. They can hear you and speak to you through an intercom. They leave the room only because the thick walls provide protection — not because you are in danger.

### 6. Treatment duration

Most courses of radiation therapy run 5 days a week (Monday to Friday) with weekends off to allow recovery. The total number of sessions varies depending on the cancer type and goal:

- Curative treatment may involve 25 to 35 sessions (5 to 7 weeks)
- Some regimens use fewer, larger doses — called hypofractionation — over 3 to 4 weeks
- Palliative treatment to relieve symptoms may involve just 1 to 10 sessions

Try not to miss sessions. If you must travel a long distance for treatment, plan your schedule in advance and speak to the social worker at the hospital about accommodation support.

### 7. Follow-up

After treatment ends, you will have regular follow-up appointments — typically at 4 to 6 weeks, then every 3 to 6 months. Your oncologist will monitor your recovery and check whether the treatment has been effective using scans and physical examination.

## Side effects and how to manage them

Side effects depend on which part of the body is treated, the dose used, and your overall health. They usually appear gradually during treatment and peak in the first 1 to 2 weeks after treatment ends, before improving.

**Fatigue** is the most common side effect. It is different from ordinary tiredness and is not relieved by sleep alone. Plan your schedule so that treatment sessions are followed by rest. Accept help from family for cooking, transport, and household tasks. Light walking, if tolerated, can reduce fatigue over time.

**Skin changes** in the treated area are common. The skin may become red, dry, tender, or itchy — similar to sunburn. Keep the area clean and moisturised with a fragrance-free lotion (your team will recommend one). Wear loose, soft clothing over the treated area. Avoid sun exposure on that skin during and after treatment.

**Eating and swallowing difficulties** can occur when radiation is given to the head, neck, chest, or abdomen. Eating soft, moist foods and drinking enough fluids is important. In hot weather — particularly in Bihar from March to June — staying hydrated is critical, as dehydration worsens fatigue and mouth irritation. Carry water, coconut water, or oral rehydration solution on treatment days. Tell your nurse or dietitian if eating becomes difficult; there are medicines and dietary strategies that help.

**Nausea** may occur if the abdomen or lower chest is in the treatment area. Anti-nausea medicines can help — take them as prescribed, even before sessions on days when you feel well.

**Hair loss in the treated area** may occur, but only in the area receiving radiation — not elsewhere on the body. Hair usually grows back after treatment, though it may take months.

**Travel fatigue and logistical strain** are real burdens, particularly for patients travelling from districts outside Patna or from other states. If possible, arrange accommodation near the treating centre for the duration of treatment so you are not making a long journey every day. Many hospitals have patient lodges or partner accommodations — ask the social worker. Caregivers should also plan rest; caregiver burnout during a 6-week course is common and worth anticipating.

## When to contact your care team

Some symptoms during radiation therapy need prompt attention. Contact your team if you notice:

- Fever of 38°C (100.4°F) or higher
- Severe skin breakdown — open sores, blistering, or significant pain in the treated area
- Significant difficulty swallowing, eating, or drinking fluids
- Severe nausea or vomiting that prevents you from keeping down food or water for more than 24 hours
- Sudden or significant pain that is new or worsening
- Any symptom that concerns you

Never adjust your treatment schedule, skip sessions, or add any herbal, Ayurvedic, or other remedies without telling your radiation oncologist first. Some substances can interact with radiation and increase side effects or reduce its effectiveness.

## Cost and financial support in India

The cost of radiation therapy varies considerably between government and private hospitals.

**Government hospitals** such as AIIMS Patna, IGIMS (Sheikhpura, Patna), Mahavir Cancer Sansthan (Phulwarisharif, Patna), Tata Memorial Centre (Mumbai), and Regional Cancer Centres offer radiation therapy at subsidised or no-cost rates for eligible patients.

**Ayushman Bharat PM-JAY** covers a range of radiation therapy treatments at empanelled hospitals, up to ₹5 lakh per family per year. Before starting, ask the hospital's Ayushman Bharat help desk whether your specific treatment is covered and what documents are needed (Aadhaar, ration card, income certificate).

**Bihar-specific note:** Mahavir Cancer Sansthan in Phulwarisharif is the largest dedicated cancer centre in Bihar and offers radiation therapy at subsidised rates. AIIMS Patna also has a radiation oncology department. IGIMS (0612-2297631) has growing oncology capacity. Call ahead to confirm equipment availability and waiting times before travelling.

## Questions to ask your radiation oncologist

Before starting treatment, you may want to ask:

1. Why is radiation therapy being recommended for my cancer, and what is the goal?
2. How many sessions will I need, and how long will each session take?
3. Which part of my body will be treated, and which nearby organs might be affected?
4. What side effects are most likely for my specific treatment, and how should I manage them?
5. Are there any activities, foods, or substances I should avoid during treatment?
6. Will I be able to work or manage my daily responsibilities during treatment?
7. What symptoms should I treat as urgent and call you about?
8. Is this treatment covered under PM-JAY, and what steps do I need to take?
9. What happens after treatment ends — how will I know if it worked?
10. Are there clinical trials or newer radiation techniques that may be relevant for my case?

Bring a family member or trusted person to your consultation to help remember what was discussed.
