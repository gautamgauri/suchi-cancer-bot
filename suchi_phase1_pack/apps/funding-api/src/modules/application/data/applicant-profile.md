### My Work (200 words)
I run two organisations from Patna, Bihar. Diksha Foundation (founded 2010) operates KHEL centres — hybrid education hubs combining digital literacy, Football3 sports coaching, and social-emotional learning — serving 476 children directly and 511 through government school partnerships. Empowering Futures reaches ~260 adolescent girls across urban Patna.

Suchitra Cancer Care Foundation (founded 2024, named after my mother) builds cancer navigation tools for patients and families. Its flagship product, Suchi Cancer Bot, answers cancer questions in plain language and guides families through the healthcare system.

Across both organisations I am building AI infrastructure that replaces what a 10-person team would otherwise do: a WhatsApp tutor (Rusty, piloted with 70 Bihar Board students via Glific), a cancer navigation bot, a KB-backed grants assistant that researches funders, scores fit, and drafts proposals. The goal is operational parity — so that a Bihar-based NGO with three staff can compete for funding and deliver programs with the same quality as an organisation ten times its size.

### AI Story (200 words)
My AI journey started not with excitement but with frustration. In 2023, I watched Diksha lose a grant we were well-suited for because we submitted a generic proposal. We didn't have time to research the funder properly. We didn't have a grants writer. The winning organisation did.

I started building the funding assistant to solve that specific problem — not as a product, but as internal infrastructure. It now researches funders via web grounding, retrieves evidence from our KB using pgvector, scores fit across six dimensions, and drafts full proposals with citations. We used it to generate our first Reliance Foundation ESA proposal in under 10 minutes.

Then came Rusty — a WhatsApp tutor for Bihar Board students built with Glific, because our students don't have laptops or stable internet but they do have phones and WhatsApp. Then Suchi Cancer Bot — because cancer patients in Bihar are navigating a terrifying system with no information and no guide.

What connects all three is the same design principle: AI that works within the actual constraints of the people it serves — low bandwidth, mixed literacy, no budget — rather than the constraints of people who can afford to ignore those things.

### Cambridge-specific (150 words)
I left Cambridge in 2016 with a research framework — the Capabilities Approach — and went back to Bihar to build the institutions that could actually realise it. Fifteen years later, Diksha has reached 2,000+ children. But I have also learned how much the gap between research and deployment matters, and how much of the tooling built for social impact assumes resources that organisations like Diksha simply do not have.

Returning to Cambridge through Digital Minds would close a loop I have been thinking about for a decade. The questions I am sitting with — how do you build AI that works within real constraints rather than ideal ones, how do you measure capability development rather than just test scores, how do you make autonomous systems trustworthy when the communities they serve have no recourse if they fail — are exactly the questions this fellowship is built around.
